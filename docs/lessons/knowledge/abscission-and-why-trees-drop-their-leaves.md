# 📚 KNOWLEDGE, SKILLS & CULTURE: Abscission and Why Trees Drop Their Leaves

## 🎯 Activity Summary

Leaves don't fall off trees. Trees cut them off. Every autumn a deciduous tree grows a special seam of cells across the base of each leaf stalk, the **abscission zone**. It pulls the useful nutrients back out of the leaf, dissolves the glue holding the seam together, and seals the wound before the leaf has even dropped. Next year's leaf is already waiting as a bud just above the cut. On a walk, children pull a leaf that isn't ready (it tears) and one that is (it pops off clean). Then they find the healed scar on the twig, with the sealed-off pipe ends and the new bud still visible, and lay out a row of leaves showing each step of the shutdown. Back home they rerun the experiment that discovered **ethylene**, the gas that tells a plant to let go.

## Vocabulary

**Abscission**: A plant deliberately shedding one of its own parts: a leaf, a petal, a ripe fruit. From Latin *ab* + *scindere*, "to cut away", the same root as *scissors*. It is an active, planned process, not things wearing out and falling off. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abscission))

**Abscission zone**: A thin band of small cells at the base of the leaf stalk, laid down months in advance. When the signal comes, enzymes dissolve the "glue" between two rows of these cells so the leaf hangs by little more than its pipes. A layer of corky, waterproof cells forms on the tree's side of the break so the wound is sealed before it opens.

**Leaf scar**: The healed mark left on a twig where a leaf used to be. Inside it are tiny dots called **bundle scars**: the sealed ends of the pipes (xylem and phloem) that used to carry water into the leaf and sugar out. On a horse chestnut twig the scar is shaped like a horseshoe, and the bundle scars sit around it like the nails. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf_scar))

**Bud**: Next year's leaf (or flower), already built and folded up tight in scales, sitting just above the leaf scar. The tree makes it in summer, before it drops the old leaf. Shedding isn't the tree giving up. It's the tree getting ready.

**Deciduous / evergreen**: A *deciduous* tree drops all its leaves for a season (from Latin *decidere*, "to fall off"). An *evergreen* keeps leaves all year. It still sheds them, just a few at a time, and its needles are narrow and waxy so they lose very little water in winter. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deciduous))

**Ethylene**: A gas made by plants that works as a hormone, a chemical message. It tells leaves to let go and fruit to ripen. A ripe banana gives it off, which is why a banana in a fruit bowl speeds up everything around it. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethylene_as_a_plant_hormone))

**Resorption**: Before cutting a leaf loose, the tree takes back the valuable parts, especially nitrogen, and stores them in the branches and roots for spring. Often about half of a leaf's nitrogen goes back into the tree. Breaking down green chlorophyll is part of that salvage job, which is why the yellow and orange underneath begins to show.

## Activity

### Activity Overview

A walk with a job: find out *how* a leaf comes off a tree. Children tug-test leaves (a green one tears, a ready one pops off clean), study the base of fallen leaves, then find the leaf scars on twigs with the bundle scars and new bud above each one. They bring back a handful of leaves and lay them out in order, from green to brown, so the row shows the shutdown step by step. At home, the ethylene experiment shows them they can make it happen themselves.

**Activity Type**: Outdoor investigation and plant-hormone experiment

**Duration**: 25-35 minutes (15-20 min walk, 10-15 min sorting and setup), plus a check-in every day for about a week

**Setup time**: 5 minutes (and one ripe banana)

**Best season**: Autumn, when trees are actively shedding. The scar-and-bud part works all winter.

### Materials Needed

**Essential**:
- A walk past a few deciduous trees or shrubs. Low branches matter more than variety: you need twigs a child can see up close
- A bag or basket for fallen leaves
- A magnifying glass (a hand lens is ideal) for leaf scars and leaf bases
- For the experiment: two clear zip-top bags or two large jars with lids, two similar leafy twigs or cuttings, and **one very ripe banana** (brown-spotted)

**Optional**:
- Printed references, prepared ahead: a labelled photo of a [leaf scar with bundle scars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf_scar) and bud; a horse chestnut twig (the "horseshoe" scar); a [microscope cross-section of an abscission zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abscission)
- A strip of card and tape for the leaf sequence
- A sticky note for each experiment bag, with the date

**Cost**: Free (plus a banana)

### Step-by-Step Instructions

**Preparation**:
1. Scout the route for one or two trees with low twigs. Horse chestnut, ash, sycamore, walnut, and lilac all have big, easy-to-read leaf scars. Note one that's mid-change (some green, some yellow leaves still on).
2. Look at a printed leaf-scar photo yourself first so you know what you're hunting for: a smooth scar below, a fat bud above, dots inside the scar.
3. Let a banana get very ripe on the counter.

**Activity Flow**:

1. **Introduction — the question** (2-3 min):
   Hold up a fallen leaf. "How did this get down here? Did the wind pull it off? Did it get old and break?" Take their answers seriously. Then: "Here's the surprise. The tree cut it off, on purpose. Trees have a way of snipping their own leaves. It's called *abscission*: cutting away. Today we're detectives. We're going to find the cut."

2. **Exploration — the tug test** (5-7 min):
   At a tree with leaves of different colours, let the child gently pull a **green** leaf. It resists, and if it comes, the stalk tears and leaves a ragged, wet end. Now pull a **yellow or brown** one that's ready. It barely needs touching, and it snaps off cleanly right at the base of the stalk.
   "The green one wasn't ready, so the tree held on tight. The yellow one: the tree had already dissolved the glue at the bottom, and it was hanging by a thread." Look at the bottom of the clean one with the magnifier: it's smooth and neat, a prepared break.
   Collect fallen leaves as you go: a few greens (if any have fallen), yellows, oranges or reds, browns.

3. **Discovery — find the scar and the bud** (6-8 min):
   Now look at the twig *where a leaf just came off*, or anywhere along a twig with a gap. With the lens:
   - The **leaf scar**: a smooth, sealed patch, already healed over. "The tree put a waterproof plaster on it *before* the leaf fell."
   - The **bundle scars**: tiny dots inside the scar. "These were the pipes. Water went up into the leaf through them, sugar came down. Now they're plugged."
   - The **bud** just above it: "And look who's waiting. That's *next year's* leaf, already made and folded up tight in its scales. The tree built it in summer."
   If you found a horse chestnut, count the "nails" in the horseshoe.
   Compare a pine or other evergreen if one is nearby: "This tree keeps its leaves. See how thin and waxy the needles are? They hardly lose any water, so they can stay."

4. **The leaf sequence** (5-7 min):
   At home, spread the fallen leaves out and ask the child to put them in order: "Which one is the beginning? Which one is the end?" Green, then green-yellow, yellow, orange or red, brown. Tape them to a card strip in a row.
   Walk along the row telling the story of the shutdown: "First the tree starts taking the good stuff back out of the leaf. It even breaks up the green so it can keep the pieces. When the green goes, the yellow that was hiding underneath shows through. Then the seam at the bottom gets ready, the pipes get plugged, and *snip*."
   This row is the display, and it shows a process happening in time, not a pile of nice-looking things.

5. **Wrap-up — the ethylene experiment** (5 min, then daily check-ins):
   Put one leafy twig in a bag or jar **with** the ripe banana, and the other in a bag or jar **without**. Seal both, label with the date, and set them side by side out of direct sun.
   "A ripe banana gives off an invisible gas called *ethylene*. Plants use ethylene as a message that means *let go*. Let's see if the banana can tell these leaves it's time."
   Check every day. Usually within a few days to a week, the twig in the banana bag yellows and drops leaves first. When a leaf falls inside the bag, look at its base. It's the same clean break you found outside.
   The history to tell when it works: in the 1800s, cities lit their streets with gas lamps, and people noticed that trees near leaky gas pipes dropped their leaves for no reason. In 1901 a young Russian scientist, [Dimitry Neljubow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethylene_as_a_plant_hormone), worked out that the culprit in the gas was ethylene. Your bag is doing the same experiment with a banana instead of a gas pipe.

### Age Adaptations

**2-3 years**:
- The tug test and the bud are the lesson. "Green leaf: *stuck*. Yellow leaf: *pop*!" is something a two-year-old can feel, and repeat for fun at every tree.
- Show the bud and call it "the baby leaf for spring". Visit the same twig again in spring to watch it open.
- Do the leaf sequence with just three leaves: green, yellow, brown.
- Words to use: *leaf, cut, scar, bud, let go*. Say *abscission* too. They'll enjoy the sound, and it's the real name.

**3-4+ years**:
- **Why cut leaves off at all?** Work it out together. In winter the ground freezes, so roots can't drink, but big flat leaves keep losing water into the air. Snow piles up on flat leaves and snaps branches. The days are too short to make much food anyway. So the tree salvages what it can from its leaves and drops them. Then ask: why can the pine keep its needles?
- **Count bundle scars** on different trees. The number and pattern are so consistent that botanists use them to [identify trees in winter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf_scar) with no leaves at all.
- **Marcescence**: look for young oaks or beeches holding onto crisp brown leaves all winter. They've skipped the final cut and let go only in spring. Nobody is completely sure why. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcescence))
- **Other things plants cut off**: a ripe apple dropping from its stalk, petals falling from a finished flower, a tomato that comes off the vine with a little "knuckle" on its stalk. That knuckle is an abscission zone; look for it at the shop.
- **Humans changed abscission**: wild wheat drops its ripe grains on the ground (called *shattering*) so its seeds spread. Early farmers kept planting the rare plants whose grains *stayed on*, because those were the only ones they could harvest. Almost every grain we eat comes from plants that lost the ability to let go. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shattering_(agriculture)))

### Extension Ideas

**Variation 1 — The Same Twig, All Year**: Tie a ribbon loosely on one low twig. Visit once a month and photograph the same spot: leaf, turning leaf, scar and bud, bud swelling, new leaf opening. Over a year that's the whole cycle, scar to bud to leaf to scar.

**Variation 2 — Petiole Snap Test, Season by Season**: Repeat the tug test in summer. Every leaf tears, because none have their seams ready. The abscission zone is there all summer but stays "locked" while the leaf is healthy; a hormone from the leaf itself (auxin) keeps it closed. As the leaf ages, that signal fades and ethylene takes over.

**Variation 3 — Banana Bag Variations**: Rerun the experiment with an apple instead of a banana (also an ethylene-maker), or with an unripe green banana (makes much less). Does the effect follow the gas?

**Repeat play**: Every walk from October to March, stop at one twig and find the scar and the bud. It becomes a habit of looking at the place where a leaf *was*, which is something most adults have never done.

## 📝 Parent/Caregiver Notes

### Common Challenges & Solutions

**Challenge**: The child wants to pull off lots of leaves.
**Solution**: Set the rule before the walk: one green tug and one yellow tug per tree. The point is the comparison, not the harvest. Fallen leaves are unlimited.

**Challenge**: The leaf scars are too small to see.
**Solution**: Pick the right tree; horse chestnut, ash, walnut, and sumac have scars the size of a fingernail. Hold the twig against the sky or a white card and use the magnifier. A printed labelled photo next to the real twig helps a lot.

**Challenge**: The ethylene experiment shows no difference.
**Solution**: It's a real experiment, so this can happen. Common reasons are that the banana isn't ripe enough, the bags aren't sealed, or the twigs are from a tree that isn't ready to shed yet. Twigs already starting to turn respond fastest. Try again with a brown-spotted banana and fresh twigs; real scientists rerun experiments too. Throw out the bags once mould appears.

**Challenge**: "Does it hurt the tree?"
**Solution**: A good question. Trees don't have nerves, so nothing hurts. But the tree *does* treat the cut like a wound and seals it up beforehand, the same way your body scabs over a scrape. The leaf-pulling we did today is so small it doesn't harm the tree; it would have let those leaves go within days anyway.

**Challenge**: No deciduous trees nearby, or it isn't autumn.
**Solution**: Houseplants do abscission too. An ageing leaf on a pothos or a ficus will come away clean with a tiny scar. A bunch of tomatoes on the vine or a stalked apple shows the abscission zone on fruit. The banana experiment works with any leafy cutting.

### Follow-Up Activities

1. **Spring revisit**: Go back to the twigs you studied and watch the buds above the scars open. Point out that the new leaf came out exactly where you predicted in October.

2. **Spot the scars everywhere**: Palm-tree trunks and many houseplant stems are ringed with old leaf scars, a record of every leaf the plant ever dropped. Count them on a palm or a yucca.

3. **Leaf colour, next lesson**: The leaf sequence leads into *where the colours come from*: the yellow pigments revealed when chlorophyll is salvaged, and the reds some trees make fresh in autumn. The chromatography "autumn reveal" in the pigments lesson shows the hidden yellow directly.
