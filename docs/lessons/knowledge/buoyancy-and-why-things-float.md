# 📚 KNOWLEDGE, SKILLS & CULTURE: Buoyancy and Why Things Float

## 🎯 Activity Summary

Water pushes *up*. On everything, always — on a cork, on a rock, on you. That upward push is **buoyancy**, and whether something floats is simply a contest between that push and the object's weight. The rule that decides the contest is **density**, not weight: a steel ship weighing 200,000 tonnes floats and a steel bolt sinks, because the ship is mostly air. Children test objects, then prove the rule the decisive way — the same piece of foil sinks as a ball and floats as a boat — and finish by discovering that air does the same thing to hot air and helium, which is why lanterns and balloons rise.

## Vocabulary

**Buoyancy**: The upward force a fluid exerts on anything placed in it. It exists because the pressure of water is higher deeper down, so water pushes up on an object's bottom harder than it presses down on its top. The leftover is a push upward. If that push is as big as the object's weight, the object floats. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buoyancy))

**Density**: How much mass is packed into a space — mass divided by volume. Water is 1 gram per cubic centimetre. Anything denser than that sinks in it; anything less dense floats. Wood is about 0.5-0.9 g/cm³ (floats), rock about 2.5 (sinks), ice 0.92 — which is why ice floats in your drink and why lakes freeze from the top down instead of the bottom up. That one oddity of water is a large part of why life survives winters.

**Archimedes' principle**: The buoyant force on an object equals the weight of the fluid it pushes out of the way. Put a boat in water, and the water it displaces weighs exactly what the boat weighs. Discovered about 2,250 years ago by [Archimedes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes%27_principle), who allegedly worked it out in the bath.

**Displace**: To push aside. When you get into a full bath, the water that overflows is the water you displaced — and its volume is exactly your volume. This is still how you measure the volume of an awkward shape.

**Average density**: The density of an object counted *including* any air it contains. This is the whole trick of shipbuilding: steel is 8 times denser than water, but a hull full of air averages out below 1, so it floats. Fish do the same thing on purpose with a **swim bladder**, a gas bag they inflate and deflate to rise and sink without swimming.

## Activity

### Activity Overview

A prediction-and-test round with household objects, then the experiment that settles it: the same piece of foil, sunk and floated. Then, if you want the full arc, the same idea carried into the air — because a hot air balloon and a boat are doing the identical thing in different fluids.

**Activity Type**: Hands-on physics experiment with water play

**Duration**: 20-30 minutes

**Setup time**: 5 minutes

### Materials Needed

**Essential**:
- A large container of water (bath, water table, or a deep bin), 4-6 inches deep
- Objects with a range of densities: cork, sponge, wooden spoon, foam ball, rock, metal spoon, coin, plastic toy, candle stub, apple, potato (the apple floats and the potato sinks — a lovely surprise)
- **Aluminium foil**, one sheet — the star of the lesson
- Towels

**Optional**:
- Kitchen scales and a measuring jug, to actually calculate density with an older child
- A clear plastic bottle and a sachet of ketchup or a pen lid with blu-tack, for the Cartesian diver
- Salt, to make water dense enough to float an egg
- Tissue paper, glue, and a hairdryer for the hot air balloon
- A helium balloon, if one is around

**Cost**: Free (uses household items)

### Step-by-Step Instructions

**Preparation**:
1. Fill the container with comfortable warm water and put towels underneath
2. Gather the test objects on a tray within reach, checking for choking hazards and sharp edges
3. Tear off a square of foil and keep it aside — don't reveal it yet
4. Decide you will not use the phrase "light things float" even once

**Activity Flow**:

1. **Introduction — water pushes up** (2-3 min):
   Hand the child something buoyant and have them push it under and hold it. "Can you feel that? The water is pushing back. Water pushes *up* on everything you put in it — that push is called *buoyancy*. It's pushing up on the rock, too. Just not hard enough."

2. **Exploration — predict and test** (8-12 min):
   One object at a time: "Float or sink?" Let them commit to a prediction before testing, then drop it in and watch. Build two piles.
   Resist explaining early — collect data first. Slip in the tricky ones: the apple (floats) and the potato (sinks); an unopened can of diet soda (floats) beside a regular one (sinks, because of the dissolved sugar); a candle; a grape.
   Say what you see in real terms: "The wooden spoon is *less dense* than water. The metal one is *more dense*."

3. **Discovery — the foil experiment** (5-8 min):
   Now the decisive test. Crumple the foil square into a tight ball. Drop it: it sinks. Fish it out, flatten it, and shape it into a shallow boat. Float it.
   Ask the question that does the work: **"Same foil. Same weight. Why did it float this time?"**
   Then give the answer properly: "Because floating isn't about weight, it's about *density* — how packed something is compared to water. The ball was solid metal. The boat is metal *plus a big pocket of air*, and when you count the air, the whole thing is less packed than water. That's *average density*."
   Push it further: load the foil boat with coins one at a time and watch it settle deeper and deeper — each coin displaces more water — until one coin too many sinks it. "Every ship in the world has a line painted on its side showing exactly how deep it's allowed to sit. That's the same experiment."
   Then Archimedes: he was asked whether the king's crown was really solid gold, without damaging it. In the bath he noticed the water rise and realized he could measure the crown's *volume* by how much water it pushed aside, and so work out its density. He shouted "Eureka!" — Greek for "I found it." (Fill a cup to the brim, submerge a rock, and catch the overflow: that puddle is the rock's volume.)

4. **Wrap-up — buoyancy in air** (3-5 min):
   "Water pushes up. Does *air* push up too?" It does — air is a fluid as well, just a thin one. Anything less dense than air floats *in air*.
   If you have a helium balloon, hold it under and let go. "Helium is less dense than air, so the air pushes it up — exactly like the cork in the water."
   And hot air is less dense than cold air, because heating spreads the same molecules over more space. That's a hot air balloon, and it is also a paper lantern: warm air rising from a flame lifts the whole thing off the ground. (See the safety note below before you fly anything.)

### Age Adaptations

**2-3 years**:
- Three or four objects is plenty; the water play matters more than the tally.
- Do the foil ball-and-boat trick anyway — it's a magic trick at this age and the words go in even when the reasoning doesn't.
- Repeat the vocabulary rhythmically: "Floating... floating... sinking... buoyant... buoyant."
- Let them hold a cork under and feel it fight back. Physical sensation of the buoyant force beats any explanation.
- Expect enormous splashing and plan for it.

**3-4+ years**:
- Actually calculate a density: weigh a rock, measure its volume by displacement, divide. Compare the number to 1.
- **Make a Cartesian diver**: fill a clear bottle to the brim, drop in a ketchup sachet (or a pen lid weighted with blu-tack) that just barely floats, and cap it tightly. Squeeze the bottle and the diver sinks; release and it rises. Squeezing compresses the little air bubble inside, making the diver denser than water. "That is exactly how a fish's swim bladder works, and how a submarine works — change how much air you're carrying and you change your density."
- **Float an egg in salt water**: an egg sinks in tap water. Stir in salt until it rises. Salt water is denser, so it pushes harder. This is why swimming in the sea is easier than in a pool, and why you cannot sink in the Dead Sea.
- Ask the icebergs question: ice floats, but only just — about 90% of an iceberg is underwater, because ice is 0.92 as dense as water. Float an ice cube and look at how little sticks out.
- Introduce the pressure explanation: water deeper down pushes harder, so the push on the bottom of an object beats the push on the top. That difference *is* the buoyant force.

### Extension Ideas

**Variation 1 — Build a Boat That Carries Cargo**: Give foil, straws, corks, and tape, and set a challenge: whose boat carries the most coins before it sinks? Then look at the winner's shape. Wide and shallow beats tall and narrow, every time, because it displaces more water for the same weight.

**Variation 2 — The Paper Lantern** (buoyancy in air): Make a tissue-paper lantern or a small tissue hot air balloon and fill it with warm air from a hairdryer held underneath — it will lift and hover. **Do not fly a lantern with a real flame**: sky lanterns start wildfires, injure livestock, and are illegal in many places. Make the lantern beautiful, fly it on hot air from the hairdryer, and hang it up afterward with a battery tealight inside.

**Variation 3 — The Density Column**: Pour honey, then washing-up liquid, then water, then oil into a tall glass. They stack in order of density and refuse to mix. Now drop in a grape, a bolt, and a plastic bead and watch each stop at its own level. Every object finds the layer where the push finally matches its weight.

**Repeat Play**: Keep a float/sink basket by the bath. Add one new object per bathtime and predict before it goes in. Over weeks, children start predicting from material rather than size — the moment they say "metal sinks, but not if it's a boat," they have the concept.

## 📝 Parent/Caregiver Notes

### Common Challenges & Solutions

**Challenge**: The child concludes "big things sink, small things float."
**Solution**: Break it deliberately with counterexamples: a huge foam pool noodle (floats), a tiny screw (sinks), the apple and the potato. Then the foil ball and boat — same size, same weight, opposite results. The wrong rule has to be beaten with evidence, not corrections.

**Challenge**: Water goes everywhere.
**Solution**: Do it in the bath or outside, and accept it. A sturdy container that won't tip, towels underneath, and a decision in advance that spillage is fine will save the session.

**Challenge**: The child wants to test the tablet, a book, or a battery toy.
**Solution**: Pre-screen the tray and keep it to what's on it. "That one's not for water — but let's test this instead." A nature walk to gather test objects redirects the impulse usefully.

**Challenge**: The child loses interest after five minutes.
**Solution**: Five minutes is a complete lesson. Do the foil trick early rather than saving it for the end, and stop while they're still enjoying it. The float/sink basket keeps the experiment running for months.

**Challenge**: An adult says "it floats because it's light."
**Solution**: Gently swap in the right sentence — "it floats because it's less dense than water" — and use the cruise ship as the reminder. This misconception is very sticky and worth heading off early.

### Follow-Up Activities

1. **Bath-time physics**: Ask the questions informally during every bath. "Will the shampoo bottle float when it's full? When it's empty?" (Full sinks, empty floats — same bottle.)

2. **Watch a boat load**: At a harbour, canal, or on video, watch how much deeper a loaded boat sits than an empty one, and find the load line painted on the hull.

3. **Ice in the drink**: Every glass of iced water is a live demonstration. "How much of the ice is under the water? Almost all of it. Same with icebergs."
