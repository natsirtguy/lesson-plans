# 📚 KNOWLEDGE, SKILLS & CULTURE: Keratin and Why Hair Is a Rope

## 🎯 Activity Summary

A hair is not a wire and not a stick. It is a **rope** — and it is built exactly the way a rope is built. A protein chain called **keratin** curls into a spiral, two spirals wind around each other, those pairs bundle and twist, those bundles bundle and twist again, and about six levels of twisting later you get something you can see: one hair, strong enough to hold up a bar of chocolate. In this lesson children build that hierarchy out of ordinary sewing thread — twisting, plying, twisting again, level by level — and then break-test every level to find out where the strength came from. It came from nowhere except the twisting.

## Vocabulary

**Protein**: A long chain of small pieces called **amino acids**, folded into a shape. The shape is the whole point — proteins do their jobs by being a particular shape. Some proteins are little machines that fold up into blobs; **keratin** is a *structural* protein, which means it folds into a long fibre and its job is simply to be strong. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein))

**Keratin**: The tough, stringy protein hair is made of — and fingernails, and the outer layer of your skin, and a cat's claws, a bird's feathers, a cow's horn, a horse's hoof, a porcupine's quill, and the great fringed sieve of baleen a whale strains its food through. A rhinoceros horn is not bone at all; it is a solid mat of hair-like keratin fibres. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keratin))

**Alpha helix**: The spiral a keratin chain twists itself into all on its own — about 3.6 amino acids per turn, like a tiny spring. [Linus Pauling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linus_Pauling) found it in 1948 while lying in bed with a cold in Oxford, folding and refolding a paper strip; he published it in 1951. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_helix))

**Coiled coil**: Two alpha helices wound around each other, like the two strands of a plied cord. [Francis Crick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Crick) proposed this shape for hair in 1952 — the year *before* he and Watson worked out DNA. Hair came first. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coiled_coil))

**Ply**: To twist strands together into something thicker and stronger. The trick that makes it work: each level twists the *opposite* way from the level below it. The two twists fight each other, and because neither can win, the rope holds itself together instead of springing apart. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rope))

**Disulfide bond**: A bridge between two sulfur atoms that pins neighbouring keratin chains to each other. Keratin is loaded with an amino acid called **cysteine**, and every cysteine carries a sulfur atom; two of them reach across the gap and lock. Thousands of those little locks are why hair does not just pull apart into fluff — and why burning hair smells so appalling. That is the sulfur. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disulfide))

## Activity

### Activity Overview

Children build a hair out of thread, one level of structure at a time, and break-test each level as they go. A single thread snaps between two fingers. The same thread, twisted and plied through five levels, will not break no matter how hard a three-year-old pulls — and no material was added. Then they test the real thing: how much weight will one human hair hold?

**Activity Type**: Hands-on model building / materials testing

**Duration**: 20-30 minutes

**Setup time**: 10 minutes

### Materials Needed

**Essential**:
- A spool of ordinary cotton sewing thread (the thinner the better — it should snap easily)
- Yarn or embroidery floss, about 2 metres, for the younger/thicker version
- Scissors
- A pencil or a chopstick (to wind a spring around, and to use as a crank handle)
- Tape
- Paperclips, coins, or small washers as weights, plus a small paper cup or a sandwich bag to hold them
- 3 or 4 long human hairs, taken from a hairbrush — **not pulled from anybody's head**
- A magnifier, or a phone camera zoomed all the way in

**Optional**:
- A short piece of real rope, cut at one end so it frays
- Unspun wool roving from a craft shop, plus warm soapy water (for the felting extension)
- A ruler, for measuring how far a hair stretches wet versus dry
- A wool jumper that shrank in a hot wash — evidence, already in the house

**Printed or pulled up ahead of time** (this lesson turns on seeing the real structures):
- A diagram of the keratin hierarchy: chain → [alpha helix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_helix) → [coiled coil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coiled_coil) → protofilament → [intermediate filament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermediate_filament) → macrofibril → cortical cell → hair
- An electron micrograph of a hair showing the overlapping [cuticle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuticle) scales
- A cross-section of a [laid rope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rope), for the side-by-side comparison
- [William Astbury's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Astbury) 1931 X-ray pictures of wool, stretched and unstretched — two different patterns from the same hair

**Cost**: Free to Under $5

### Step-by-Step Instructions

**Preparation**:
1. Collect 3 or 4 long hairs from a hairbrush and tape one end of each to the edge of a table so they hang free.
2. Cut about eight 60 cm lengths of sewing thread. Short lengths tangle far less than long ones.
3. Print or open the reference images.
4. **Safety check**: thread and hair should never go around a neck, and should never be wound tight around a finger. Keep lengths short, stay at the table, and collect every scrap at the end.

**Activity Flow**:

1. **Introduction — a hair is a rope** (3 min):
   Hold up a single hair against the light. "This isn't a stick and it isn't a wire. It's a rope. And it's dead — there's nothing alive in it at all, which is why a haircut doesn't hurt. Only the little factory at the bottom, the *follicle*, is alive, and it squeezes hair out like toothpaste out of a tube, about a centimetre every month."
   Then: "One single hair from your head can hold up a bar of chocolate. If you tied every hair on your head to something heavy — about a hundred thousand hairs — they could between them hold up two elephants."
   Now hand over a single sewing thread. "Pull it." It snaps immediately. "Good. That's about as strong as one keratin chain. Let's build a hair out of it."

2. **Exploration — building the levels** (10-14 min):
   Go up the hierarchy one step at a time, and name each step as you make it.
   - **Level 1 — the chain.** One thread, lying flat. Snaps. "One protein chain, all by itself. Useless."
   - **Level 2 — the alpha helix.** Wind a thread tightly up the pencil so it spirals, and leave it there. "A keratin chain does this on its own. It curls into a spiral called an *alpha helix*, three and a bit pieces per turn." Now slide it off: the thread flops instantly, and say why. "Ours falls apart because nothing is holding the turns together. A real one has thousands of tiny grips called *hydrogen bonds*, each turn clipped to the turn below it, so the spring holds. That is also the bit that water can undo — remember that when we curl a wet hair later."
   - **Level 3 — the coiled coil.** Take a doubled thread, hold both ends, and twist as hard as you can in one direction until it starts to fight you. Now bring the two ends together and let the middle go — it whips around itself into a two-ply cord, twisting the *opposite* way. "Look at what it just did. Nobody twisted it that way. A twisted thing *wants* to coil back on itself. That's a *coiled coil*, and that is exactly what keratin does: two spirals wound around each other."
   - **Level 4 — the filament.** Two coiled-coil cords, twisted hard together, plied again. "Now it's a *filament*. In a real hair it takes thirty-two chains to make one of these, and it's still far too thin to see."
   - **Level 5 — the macrofibril, and the hair.** Four filaments, twisted and plied one more time. "Bundle these into cells, pack the cells together, wrap the whole thing in scales, and you have a hair."
   The rule to repeat every single time: **each level twists the opposite way to the one below it.** "That's the whole secret. Each twist is trying to undo the other one. Neither one can win, so it holds."

3. **Discovery — the tests** (6-8 min):
   - **The break test.** Line up your one-ply, two-ply, four-ply and eight-ply and pull each one. Where do they stop breaking? Hand the thick one to the child and invite them to break it. They can't. "Nothing was added. It's the same thread. All we did was twist it."
   - **The hair test.** Hang the paper cup from a taped-up single hair and drop in paperclips or coins one at a time, counting aloud. Most hairs take a surprising load before they go — somewhere near 100 grams. Then do the same with one sewing thread. The hair usually wins, and that result should be allowed to be astonishing.
   - **The stretch test.** Hold a dry hair against the ruler, pull gently, and let go — a little stretch, then it springs back. Now wet it and pull again. Wet hair stretches enormously, up to half as long again, and still recovers. "In 1931 a scientist called William Astbury fired X-rays at wool, and then at *stretched* wool, and got two completely different pictures. That's how people first found out there was a coiled-up spring in there that could be pulled straight."
   - **The split-end test.** Hunt through the brush hairs with the magnifier for a split end, and put it next to the frayed cut end of the rope. They are the same picture. "That's your hair's rope coming unravelled. A split end is the structure showing itself."

4. **Wrap-up — keratin everywhere** (3 min):
   Go around the house and the animal kingdom naming it: fingernails and toenails, the top dead layer of your own skin, the dog's claws, a bird's feather and its beak, a cow's horn, a horse's hoof, a tortoise's shell plates, a porcupine's quill, the whiskers on a cat. A [rhinoceros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_(anatomy)) horn is nothing but a solid mat of the stuff — hair, compressed and glued. A whale's [baleen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baleen) is a comb of it, hundreds of keratin plates fraying into bristles that strain shrimp out of the sea.
   Finish on the sulfur: "Keratin is stuffed with an amino acid called *cysteine*, and cysteine carries a sulfur atom. Two of them reach across and hold hands — that's a *disulfide bond*. Thousands and thousands of tiny locks holding the ropes to each other. It's why hair is so tough. And it's why burning hair smells so terrible: that's the sulfur getting out."

### Age Adaptations

**2-3 years**:
- The twisting *is* the lesson. Use yarn, not thread — thick, colourful, forgiving, and it plies beautifully.
- Do the "twist hard, then bring the ends together and let go" trick over and over. Watching a cord ply itself is genuine magic at this age, and it is the actual concept.
- Two levels is plenty. Single strand → snaps. Fat plied cord → doesn't.
- Say **keratin** every single time you say hair. That's the deposit being made.
- Hang paperclips off a hair together and count them out loud. Counting to seven and then hearing a *ping* is a good afternoon.

**3-4+ years**:
- Build the full ladder and lay the levels out in a row, thinnest to thickest, as a staircase. Count them: chain, helix, coiled coil, protofilament, filament, macrofibril, cell, hair.
- Give the twist directions their real names: a strand twisted clockwise is **Z-twist**, anticlockwise is **S-twist**, and a proper rope alternates them at every level. Check which way each of your cords went.
- Make the hair test quantitative. Weigh the cup on a kitchen scale when the hair finally breaks and write the number down. Test a thick hair against a fine one. Test a hair from a different head.
- **The curl experiment.** Wet a long hair, wind it tightly around the pencil, leave it to dry completely, then slide it off — it stays curled. Now wet it again and the curl falls straight out. "Water breaks the weak little bonds — *hydrogen bonds* — and when it dries they re-form wherever the hair happens to be sitting. That's every curler and every blow-dry. To make a curl that *doesn't* wash out you have to break the strong bonds, the disulfide ones, with chemicals, and rebuild them in the new shape. That's a [perm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perm_(hairstyle))."
- Ask why some people's hair is curly with no curlers at all: the follicle's own shape. A round hole extrudes a round, straight hair; a flattened oval hole extrudes a flat, ribbon-like hair that curls.

### Extension Ideas

**Variation 1 — Felting, and why jumpers shrink**: A hair is wrapped in overlapping scales — the **cuticle** — all pointing towards the tip, like roof tiles. Run a hair between finger and thumb from root to tip, then from tip to root: one way is smooth, the other catches. Now take a puff of wool roving, wet it with warm soapy water, and rub it between your palms for two minutes. It turns into solid felt, and it will never be wool again. The scales ratchet past each other and can't go back. This is why a wool jumper in a hot wash comes out doll-sized, and how [felt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felt) — probably the oldest fabric humans made — gets made without any weaving at all.

**Variation 2 — Make a real rope**: Tie one end of a long string to a doorknob, put a pencil through the other end, and wind until the whole length is humming tight. Fold it in half and let it ply. Do it again with three strands for a proper three-strand laid rope. Then cut the end and watch it fray — into strands, into yarns, into fibres. You are taking the hierarchy back apart.

**Variation 3 — The other kind of keratin**: Hair is **alpha**-keratin: coiled, springy, stretchy. Feathers and reptile scales are **beta**-keratin: flat pleated sheets, harder and stiffer, with almost no stretch. [Spider silk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spider_silk) is built on flat sheets too, which is why it's stronger than steel by weight and yet nothing like a spring. Stretch a piece of wool yarn and a piece of silk ribbon side by side and feel the difference between a coil and a sheet.

**Variation 4 — Weaving, the other way to be strong**: Twisting is one way to make many weak fibres into one strong thing. **Weaving** is the other — over, under, over, under, so every thread is trapped by its neighbours. Weave paper strips or yarn on a cardboard loom and pull the result. Hair does the first; cloth does the second; a rope-plus-woven-sheath climbing rope does both at once.

**Repeat play**: Keep a jar of plied cords and add to it. Ply two colours together and you have a friendship bracelet; ply four and you have a keychain. Every one of them is a keratin model.

## 📝 Parent/Caregiver Notes

### Common Challenges & Solutions

**Challenge**: The child's hands aren't strong or coordinated enough to twist thread tightly.
**Solution**: Switch to yarn, and let them turn a pencil as a crank while you hold the far end — or tie the far end to a doorknob. They only need to be present for the moment of release, when the cord plies itself. That's the moment that matters.

**Challenge**: The thread turns into a knot the instant it's handled.
**Solution**: Shorter pieces (40-60 cm) and thicker yarn. Cut a knot out and carry on rather than picking at it; nothing in this lesson depends on any particular piece of string surviving.

**Challenge**: The child wants to pull a hair out of their own head, or yours.
**Solution**: Hairbrush hairs only, and say why: "It doesn't hurt to cut hair because hair is dead, but the bit at the bottom is alive and pulling *that* hurts." Have four hairs ready before you start so there's no reason to go hunting.

**Challenge**: The hair won't break and the weight test stalls.
**Solution**: That is the finding, and it is the good one. Say the number out loud and switch to heavier things — a small spoon, a key, a satsuma. If it still holds, tie two hairs together and try to snap those; a hair losing to nothing in the kitchen is a better result than a hair snapping on cue.

**Challenge**: It turns into free-form string play about four minutes in.
**Solution**: Let it. Do the single-thread snap and one twist-and-ply first — those two take ninety seconds and they carry the whole idea — and then hand over the yarn. Come back to the hair-weight test later in the day when the string has been forgotten.

**Challenge**: "Why does it matter that hair is a rope?"
**Solution**: Because almost everything strong and flexible is built this way, and once you've seen it once you see it everywhere: rope, cable, tendon, muscle fibre, spider silk, the steel cables holding up a bridge. Nature and engineers found the same answer separately. Point at a suspension bridge cable next time you cross one.

### Follow-Up Activities

1. **Spot keratin for a week.** Fingernails at bath time, the cat's claws, a feather on the pavement, a hoof at the farm, the horn on a picture of a cow. Say the word every time; the child will start pointing them out unprompted within days.

2. **Watch keratin grow.** Hair is hard to mark, but a fingernail isn't: file a tiny notch at the base of one and write down the date. Watch it travel out towards the tip over the following weeks — the same keratin factory, laying down about three millimetres a month. It is the slowest and most reliable experiment in the house.

3. **Look at frayed things.** A shoelace with the plastic tip gone, a rope end, a worn cuff, the towel after a wash. Everything that frays is a hierarchy coming apart, and now there's a word for what you're looking at.
