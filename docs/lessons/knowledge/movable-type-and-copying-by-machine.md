# 📚 KNOWLEDGE, SKILLS & CULTURE: Movable Type and Copying by Machine

## 🎯 Activity Summary

Before about 1450, every book in Europe was written out by hand, one letter at a time, by a person. A single Bible took a scribe over a year. Then Johannes Gutenberg had an idea that was not "printing" — the Chinese had been printing from carved woodblocks for 1,200 years — but **movable type**: cast every letter as a separate little metal block, so the same letters can be rearranged into any page, any book, forever. Within fifty years, Europe had millions of books. This lesson gives children a set of stamps and the two discoveries that come with them: a printed letter comes out **backwards**, so type must be cut in mirror; and once you have the blocks, the tenth copy costs almost nothing — which is the reason the world changed.

## Vocabulary

**Movable type**: Individual reusable pieces of type, one per character, which can be arranged into a page, printed, taken apart, and rearranged into a completely different page. Invented in clay by [Bi Sheng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bi_Sheng) in China around 1040, cast in bronze in Korea (the [Jikji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jikji), 1377, is the oldest surviving book printed with metal type), and developed in Europe by [Johannes Gutenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Gutenberg) around 1450. The reusability is the whole invention. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movable_type))

**Relief printing**: Printing from a raised surface — the high parts take the ink and print, the cut-away parts stay blank. A potato stamp, a woodblock, and a piece of metal type all work this way, and it is the oldest printing method there is. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relief_printing))

**Mirror image**: What comes off any relief block: left and right swap. So every letter on a piece of type is cut backwards, and the printer reads their page in reverse all day. It's why "p" and "q" were easy to mix up in the type case — hence "mind your p's and q's."

**Impression**: One press of block onto paper — and the reason "printing" and "pressure" are related words. Gutenberg adapted a **screw press** of the kind used for crushing grapes and olives, because the problem was never the ink, it was applying even, heavy force across a whole page at once.

**Edition**: All the copies made from one setting of type. This is a genuinely new kind of object in history: thousands of things that are *identical*. Once identical copies exist, two people far apart can read exactly the same words and argue about the same page number.

## Activity

### Activity Overview

Children carve or assemble a small set of reusable letter stamps, discover the mirror problem the hard way, rearrange the same letters into different words, and then race hand-copying against printing to find out for themselves why one won.

**Activity Type**: Making + a timed experiment

**Duration**: 25-35 minutes

**Setup time**: 10 minutes (carving the potato letters)

### Materials Needed

**Essential**:
- Potatoes (or thick craft-foam shapes stuck to bottle caps — much easier, and reusable)
- A small sharp knife for the adult to carve with
- Washable paint or a stamp pad, in a shallow dish
- Paper — lots of small sheets, because the point is many copies
- A pencil and paper for the hand-copying race
- A clock or phone timer

**Optional**:
- Ready-made alphabet stamps, if you have them — this whole lesson works brilliantly with a cheap set
- A tray or shallow box lid to hold the type in order (a rudimentary **type case**)
- A rolling pin or a heavy book to press with, for even impressions
- Printed references: a page of the [Gutenberg Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gutenberg_Bible), a [type case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_case) full of sorts, an illuminated [manuscript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illuminated_manuscript) page for comparison

**Cost**: Free to Under $5

### Step-by-Step Instructions

**Preparation**:
1. Carve three or four letters into potato halves — **carve them mirrored**, but don't mention it yet. Choose letters that make more than one word: A, C, T, S gives CAT, ACT, CATS, SAT, TACS
2. Deliberately carve one letter the *right* way round, forwards. It will print backwards, and that mistake is the best teaching moment in the lesson
3. Set out paint in a shallow dish, and a stack of small paper sheets
4. Tape up the printed references if you have them

**Activity Flow**:

1. **Introduction — the year of copying** (3-4 min):
   Hold up any book. "Before about 500 years ago, every single book in Europe was written out by hand. Somebody sat in a cold room with a feather pen and copied this whole thing, word by word. A big book took more than a year. That's why a book cost as much as a house, and why almost nobody owned one."
   Then the invention: "People had figured out how to print from a carved block of wood — carve a whole page, print it lots of times. But if you wanted a different page, you carved a whole new block. Then somebody had a much better idea: make every *letter* a separate little block. Then you can shuffle them into any page you like, forever. That's called *movable type*."

2. **Exploration — printing, and the backwards problem** (8-10 min):
   Let them ink and press each letter. Press firmly and lift straight up.
   Now bring out the letter you carved forwards. Print it. It comes out backwards. Hold the stamp and the print side by side.
   Ask: "Why did that happen?" Then show it physically — press a palm onto the table and point out that the side that touches is the side you *can't* see; the stamp meets the paper face to face, like two people shaking hands, so left meets right.
   Say the consequence: "So every letter on every piece of type in history had to be made *backwards*. Printers spent their whole lives reading everything in a mirror." Then fix the potato together — recarve it mirrored — and print it again correctly. That repair is worth more than any explanation.

3. **Discovery — rearrange the type** (5-7 min):
   Line up the letters in a tray. Print CAT. Now, *without making anything new*, print ACT. Then CATS. Then SAT.
   "Look what just happened. You made four different words and you didn't carve anything new. A woodblock would have needed four blocks. This is why movable type beat woodblocks: the letters get *reused*."
   Then set a page: print the child's name, over and over down the sheet, as a nameplate for their door.

4. **Wrap-up — the race** (5-8 min):
   The experiment that explains history. Pick a short word. **Round 1**: the child (or you) writes it by hand ten times, carefully, as a scribe would. Time it. **Round 2**: stamp it ten times with the type. Time it.
   Compare the two numbers out loud, then scale them up: "Now imagine a whole book. Now imagine a thousand books." Also compare the *look*: the handwritten ones are all slightly different; the printed ones are identical. "Before printing, no two copies of anything were exactly the same. Every scribe made small mistakes, and the mistakes got copied by the next scribe, and by the next."
   Land it: "Within fifty years of Gutenberg, Europe went from a few thousand books to millions. Ordinary people learned to read because there was suddenly something to read. Almost everything that happened after that — science, revolutions, newspapers, schools — runs through that one machine."

### Age Adaptations

**2-3 years**:
- Stamping is the activity. Dip, press hard, lift. Do it fifty times if they want to.
- Use big chunky shapes rather than letters — a star, a circle, a hand — because the *repeat* is the concept and letters aren't needed for it.
- Do one thing deliberately: print the same stamp in a row and count the copies. "One... two... three... all the same!"
- Show the mirror effect with their own hand: paint a palm, press it, and hold the print up beside their hand. It's a mirror, and hands make the point without words.
- Use the words *print*, *press*, *copy*, *the same* constantly.

**3-4+ years**:
- Do the mirror lesson properly with a letter from their own name, and check the print in an actual mirror to see it come right again.
- Introduce the **type case**: a real printer's case has a compartment per letter, with the most-used letters (e, t, a) in the biggest bins nearest the hand. Set up a tray this way and ask which letters they'd want closest. That's a lesson about frequency, and about designing tools around how they get used.
- Compare **woodblock vs movable type** directly: carve a whole short word into a single potato slab, and set the same word from separate letters. Both print the same. Now ask for a *different* word. One of them can do it and one can't.
- Count the cost: a Gutenberg Bible needed around 46,000 individual pieces of type set by hand, and roughly 100,000 individual sorts were cast. Setting one page took a skilled compositor hours. "Fast printing, very slow setting" — the same trade-off as the mold in casting: hard once, easy forever.
- Talk about what got printed and what happened next: bibles, then news-sheets, then pamphlets, then arguments. Rulers who liked their people not reading things tried to control presses almost immediately.

### Extension Ideas

**Variation 1 — Mirror Writing**: Write a message in mirror writing and read it in a bathroom mirror. Leonardo da Vinci wrote his notebooks this way for decades. It's difficult, funny, and it makes the printer's daily reality vivid.

**Variation 2 — Print a Real Edition**: Decide on something worth many copies — an invitation, a party menu, a wrapping paper, a sign for a bedroom door — and print an *edition* of ten. Number them 1/10 to 10/10 the way printmakers do. The child now owns an edition.

**Variation 3 — Fabric and Repeat Patterns**: With fabric paint, print a repeating block pattern onto a tote bag or a pillowcase. This is how printed cloth was made for centuries, block by block, and it turns the "reuse the block" idea into something wearable.

**Repeat play**: Foam-on-bottle-cap letters survive washing and live in a box for months. Bring them out for cards, labels, and signs; the alphabet slowly gets used, and the mirror rule gets rediscovered every time someone carves a new one.

## 📝 Parent/Caregiver Notes

### Common Challenges & Solutions

**Challenge**: The prints are faint or smudged.
**Solution**: Too much paint smudges; too little prints faint. Blot the stamp on scrap paper once after inking, press firmly straight down, and lift without sliding. A book pressed on top gives even pressure — which is exactly the problem Gutenberg solved with a screw press.

**Challenge**: The child is frustrated that the letters come out backwards.
**Solution**: That frustration *is* the lesson — say so, and say that every printer for 500 years had to solve it. Fix one letter together and reprint. Checking the wrong print in a mirror to see it come right is the satisfying finish.

**Challenge**: Potatoes are slippery, awkward, and go off.
**Solution**: Craft foam glued to bottle caps is better in every way except romance: easier to cut, easier to grip, reusable indefinitely. Use potatoes for one memorable letter and foam for the set.

**Challenge**: The child wants to stamp randomly rather than spell.
**Solution**: Let them, then bring it back with one question: "Can you print the same thing twice exactly?" The idea being taught is repeatability, and it survives a chaotic page.

**Challenge**: Carving letters accurately is beyond the adult.
**Solution**: It is genuinely hard, which is worth admitting. Use simple straight-line letters (T, L, E, H, I) or a cheap alphabet stamp set. The lesson is about rearranging and repeating, not about carving quality.

### Follow-Up Activities

1. **Look for print everywhere**: Cereal boxes, labels, receipts, road signs, the book at bedtime. Ask each time: how many copies of this exist? Who made the first one?

2. **Visit type in the wild**: Museums with a working letterpress will often let a child pull a print. It is unforgettable, and the smell of ink comes with it.

3. **Compare a handwritten and printed page**: Look at an illuminated manuscript beside a printed page. Notice what was gained (many, cheap, identical) and what was lost (gold leaf, ornament, one-of-a-kind).
