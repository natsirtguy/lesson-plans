# 📚 KNOWLEDGE, SKILLS & CULTURE: Murals and Painting on Walls

## 🎯 Activity Summary

The oldest paintings humans have ever made are on walls, in the dark, deep inside caves — and people have never stopped. A **mural** is painting that can't be bought, moved, or hung above a sofa: it belongs to the wall, which means it belongs to everyone who walks past. This lesson traces that one continuous habit across 40,000 years — Chauvet's lions, the hand stencils of Cueva de las Manos, Pompeii's frescoes, the Sistine ceiling, Diego Rivera's factory workers, Keith Haring's chalk figures in the subway — and then puts printed examples on the wall and has the child paint in each style, one panel at a time, using each culture's actual technique.

## Vocabulary

**Mural**: A painting made directly on a wall or ceiling. From Latin *murus*, "wall." Because you can't take it away, a mural is made for whoever happens to be there — it is the oldest form of public art. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mural))

**Fresco**: Painting into *wet plaster*, so that as the plaster dries the color becomes part of the wall itself rather than a skin sitting on top. It cannot be corrected once it sets, so the painter has to finish each patch in a single day — the day's patch is called a *giornata*, Italian for "a day's work." Michelangelo painted the Sistine ceiling this way, on his back, one giornata at a time. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresco))

**Hand stencil**: The oldest signature in the world. Place a hand flat against rock, then blow or spray pigment over it and lift — the wall keeps the shape of the hand, in negative. There are hundreds of them at [Cueva de las Manos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cueva_de_las_Manos) in Argentina, made about 9,000 years ago, and many are children's hands. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand_stencil))

**Public art**: Art placed where people live rather than where art is kept. Nobody buys a ticket. That changes what it's for: murals usually argue, remember, celebrate, or warn — they are talking to a whole street at once.

**Muralismo**: The Mexican mural movement of the 1920s–50s. After a revolution, the new government hired artists — [Diego Rivera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diego_Rivera), José Clemente Orozco, David Alfaro Siqueiros — to paint the country's history on the walls of public buildings, deliberately, so that people who couldn't read could see it. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_muralism))

## Activity

### Activity Overview

A gallery goes up on the wall first — four printed murals from four times and places. Then the child gets a long strip of paper and paints one panel per style, using the technique that style actually used: blown pigment for the cave, flat profile figures for Egypt, big rounded bodies in bold color for Rivera, thick single-weight outlines for Haring. The point is comparison: same wall, same paint, four completely different ways humans have decided a picture should look.

**Activity Type**: Art history + technique replication

**Duration**: 30-45 minutes (or split across days, one style per session)

**Setup time**: 15 minutes, plus printing beforehand

### Materials Needed

**Essential**:
- A long roll of paper (lining paper, butcher paper, or taped-together sheets), taped to a wall — **on the wall, not the table**; painting vertically is half the experience
- Washable tempera or poster paint: red/ochre, black, white, yellow, blue
- Wide brushes and one thin brush for outlines
- A sponge or an old toothbrush for spatter/stencil work
- Masking tape

**Essential — printed ahead of time** (tape these up beside the paper; the lesson depends on them):
- [Cueva de las Manos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cueva_de_las_Manos) — hundreds of hand stencils
- [Chauvet Cave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chauvet_Cave) or [Lascaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lascaux) — the horses, lions, and bulls
- An Egyptian tomb wall — e.g. [Tomb of Nebamun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_Nebamun) — figures in profile, arranged in bands
- [Detroit Industry Murals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit_Industry_Murals) by Diego Rivera
- [Keith Haring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Haring) subway drawings
- Optional extras for breadth: [Ajanta Caves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ajanta_Caves) (India), [Villa of the Mysteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa_of_the_Mysteries) (Pompeii), [Sistine Chapel ceiling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sistine_Chapel_ceiling)

**Optional**:
- Plaster of Paris or ready-mixed filler and a scrap board, for the real fresco demonstration
- A spray bottle or a drinking straw for blown pigment (a straw is closer to the original method — but see the safety note below)
- Ochre-colored earth or turmeric to make the cave panel from genuine earth pigment
- A torch, to look at the cave panel the way it was meant to be seen

**Cost**: Under $10

### Step-by-Step Instructions

**Preparation**:
1. Print the reference images and tape them at the child's eye level, beside the blank paper
2. Tape the long paper to a wall (protect the wall behind it with an old sheet or a bin bag)
3. Divide the paper into four panels with a strip of masking tape between each
4. Mix paint thin enough to spatter for the stencil panel; thick for the rest
5. Dress everyone for disaster

**Activity Flow**:

1. **Introduction — the gallery walk** (5 min):
   Walk the printed images together, oldest first, and give each one sentence:
   "These horses were painted on a cave wall in France about 30,000 years ago, by firelight, deep underground where nobody lived. Nobody knows exactly why."
   "These hands are 9,000 years old, in Argentina. A lot of them are children's hands. Somebody put their hand on a rock and blew paint over it, and it's still there."
   "These Egyptians are painted on a tomb wall — always faces sideways, shoulders forward, everyone lined up in rows."
   "This one is a factory, painted in 1933 on the walls of an art museum in Detroit, by a Mexican painter called Diego Rivera. He painted workers as giants."
   "And this is Keith Haring, drawing in a New York subway station in the 1980s, fast, in chalk, on advertising boards, for whoever walked past."
   Then the frame: "All of these are *murals* — paintings on walls. You can't take one home. It belongs to the wall, so it belongs to everybody who walks by."

2. **Panel 1 — the cave: hand stencils** (8-10 min):
   The child holds one hand flat against the paper. Spatter or flick thin ochre paint over it with a toothbrush or sponge (or blow it through a straw held *beside* the mouth — never with paint in the mouth). Lift the hand: a negative handprint, made exactly the way it was made 9,000 years ago.
   "You didn't paint your hand. You painted *around* it. The wall keeps the shape of where your hand was."
   Add an animal in ochre and charcoal-black, using the cave trick: follow a bump or a fold in the paper (crumple it first) so the shape of the surface becomes the shape of the animal's back. Cave painters did that with the rock.

3. **Panel 2 — Egypt: rules for pictures** (5-7 min):
   Look closely at the tomb painting. Ask what's odd: everyone's face is sideways but their body faces us; the important person is drawn much bigger than the servants; the scene runs in stacked bands.
   "These aren't mistakes. They're rules. Egyptian painters kept the same rules for three thousand years — longer than the time between now and the Romans." Paint a figure by those rules: head sideways, eye front, shoulders square, feet sideways, big for important.

4. **Panel 3 — Rivera: big bodies, bold color** (5-7 min):
   Point at the workers: rounded, heavy, solid, filling the space; flat strong colors; everybody *doing* something.
   "Rivera painted ordinary working people the size of gods, on a wall in a public building, so anyone could walk in and see themselves as important." Paint someone the family knows — doing their real job — huge, rounded, in three flat colors.

5. **Panel 4 — Haring: outline only** (5 min):
   One color, one thick brush, no shading, no faces — just outlines that move. Speed is part of the style: Haring drew in subway stations quickly because the drawings were unofficial. Set a two-minute timer and let the child fill the panel with outlined figures, dogs, and radiating lines.

6. **Wrap-up — compare and choose** (3-5 min):
   Step back and look at all four panels together. "Same paint. Same wall. Same kid. Four completely different ways of drawing a person. Which rules do you like best?"
   Then the fresco demonstration, if you have plaster: spread a patch of wet plaster or filler on a board, paint into it while it's wet, and paint another patch after it dries. Come back tomorrow and scratch both with a fingernail. The wet-plaster paint is *in* the wall; the dry-plaster paint flakes off. "That's why 500-year-old frescoes are still bright, and why Michelangelo had to finish each patch in one day, before it dried."

### Age Adaptations

**2-3 years**:
- Hand stencils, and lots of them. Repeat until the whole panel is covered — this is what the real cave looks like anyway.
- Skip the four-style structure; do the cave panel only, and look at the printed pictures between painting bouts.
- Use two colors: ochre and black. That's most of what cave painters had.
- Say "mural" every time and point at the wall: "It's on the *wall*. That makes it a mural."
- Do it by torchlight with the room lights off for two minutes. The effect is immediate.

**3-4+ years**:
- Do all four panels, and add the question of *why* each mural was made: to record a hunt? to bury a king? to tell factory workers they matter? to make a subway less grey?
- Introduce the **grid transfer** method that muralists actually use: draw a small picture on paper, rule it into squares, rule matching bigger squares on the big paper, and copy one square at a time. Suddenly a small drawing becomes a wall-sized one, which is a genuinely thrilling piece of technology.
- Talk about the [Sistine Chapel ceiling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sistine_Chapel_ceiling): four years, on scaffolding, painting upward. Tape paper to the underside of a table and have them paint lying on their back for five minutes.
- Raise the street-art question honestly: Haring drew on advertising boards he wasn't supposed to use, and got arrested for it. Some murals are commissioned and some are not. "Whose wall is it? Does it matter if the painting is good?" Then agree the household rule: our paper, not our walls.
- Look for local murals on a walk and photograph them. Every city has some.

### Extension Ideas

**Variation 1 — The Cave Experience**: Tape paper under a table or behind a sofa, turn off the lights, and paint by torch only. Cave painters worked by animal-fat lamps in absolute darkness, hundreds of meters underground, in places nobody slept. Painting cramped and half-blind changes what you make.

**Variation 2 — A Real Fresco Tile**: Spread plaster of Paris in a shallow lid, and while it's still wet paint into it with earth pigment. It sets into a stone tile with the picture inside it. Bang it out of the lid when hard — that's a fragment of fresco, made the Pompeii way.

**Variation 3 — The Family Story Wall**: Plan a mural before painting it, the way muralists do: what story does it tell, who's in it, what happens on the left and what happens on the right? Rivera's murals read like sentences across the wall. Sketch it small, then grid it up.

**Repeat play**: Keep the paper roll taped up for a week and let the mural grow — add a panel a day, invite other family members to contribute their own figure, and photograph it at each stage. Murals are almost never made alone or in one sitting.

## 📝 Parent/Caregiver Notes

### Common Challenges & Solutions

**Challenge**: The child paints on the actual wall.
**Solution**: Predictable, given the subject. Cover the wall behind the paper, extend the paper wider than you think you need, and say the rule out loud before you start: "Murals go on walls. *Our* mural goes on this paper, because this wall belongs to all of us." If it happens, wash it together — that's what mural conservators do.

**Challenge**: Blowing paint through a straw gets paint in the mouth.
**Solution**: Use a toothbrush flick or a spray bottle instead — same negative-stencil effect, no risk. If you do use a straw, hold it so it never touches the lips and blow *across* the top of a paint pot rather than sucking anything up.

**Challenge**: The child hates the "rules" panels and wants to paint freely.
**Solution**: Let them. Do the two panels they enjoy and leave the others blank for another day. The comparison still works with two, and the reference pictures on the wall keep working even when nobody's copying them.

**Challenge**: Four styles is too much and everything turns into brown mud.
**Solution**: One style per session. Spread it over a week. Brown mud is also honest — it's what happens when every pigment mixes, and it's exactly what most cave palettes look like now.

**Challenge**: The child asks whether the cave people were "good at drawing."
**Solution**: Look together at the Chauvet horses. They're extraordinary — shaded, foreshortened, alive. The honest answer is yes, astonishingly, 30,000 years ago, with no school, no galleries, and no shops.

### Follow-Up Activities

1. **Hunt murals on the walk**: Photograph every painted wall you pass for a week — shop shutters, underpasses, community murals, ghost signs. Ask each time: who paid for this, and who is it talking to?

2. **Look up your own town's murals**: Many places have a mural trail. Wikipedia and local council pages usually list them.

3. **Handprints as a family record**: Do a hand stencil panel every birthday. The Cueva de las Manos hands are still legible after 9,000 years; yours will show a hand getting bigger year by year.
