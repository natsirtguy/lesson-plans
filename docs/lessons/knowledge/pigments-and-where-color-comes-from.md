# 📚 KNOWLEDGE, SKILLS & CULTURE: Pigments and Where Color Comes From

## 🎯 Activity Summary

Almost every color you have ever seen is a **pigment**: a molecule that swallows some wavelengths of light and spits the rest back at your eyes. The green of a leaf is chlorophyll. The orange of a carrot — and of a flamingo — is carotene. The red of blood is haemoglobin, built around an iron atom. The brown of your own skin is melanin. The red on a cave wall in France is rust. And a pigment is only half of paint: the other half is the **binder**, the glue that makes the color stay put. In this lesson children grind real pigments out of the kitchen — charcoal, turmeric, paprika, spinach, blueberries, dirt — mix them with four different binders, and find out which combinations make paint and which make a mess that washes off.

## Vocabulary

**Pigment**: A substance whose molecules absorb some wavelengths of light and reflect others, so it looks colored. A red pigment is red because it *absorbs* blue and green light and throws red back to your eye. There is no red inside it — there is red *left over*. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pigment))

**Binder**: The sticky part of paint that carries the pigment and glues it to the surface. Change the binder and you change everything about the paint: egg yolk makes tempera (medieval altarpieces), linseed oil makes oil paint (Rembrandt), gum arabic makes watercolor, acrylic polymer makes acrylics, and wet lime plaster makes fresco. Cave painters used spit, animal fat, and blood.

**Chlorophyll**: The green pigment inside plant cells that absorbs red and blue light to power photosynthesis — and reflects the green it can't use. Leaves are green because green is the color a leaf *rejects*. In autumn, trees shut down chlorophyll production and the orange and yellow carotenes that were there all along finally show. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll))

**Structural color**: Color made with no pigment at all — from microscopic ridges that bounce light waves into each other so only one color survives. A blue morpho butterfly has no blue pigment; crush its wing to powder and the blue is gone. Peacocks, beetles, and blue jays work the same way. There is almost no true blue pigment in the animal world, so animals cheat with architecture. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_coloration))

**Ochre**: Clay colored by iron oxide — plain rust — in reds, yellows, and browns. It is the oldest paint humans have. People were grinding ochre 100,000 years before they invented writing, and the handprints and bulls at [Lascaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lascaux) are made of it.

## Activity

### Activity Overview

Children make paint from scratch. First they extract pigment from ordinary kitchen materials by grinding, crushing, and steeping; then they split each pigment between four binders and paint identical swatches; then — after drying — they run the test that separates paint from colored dust: rub it, and splash it with water.

**Activity Type**: Materials chemistry / hands-on making

**Duration**: 25-35 minutes, plus a drying gap before the rub test

**Setup time**: 10 minutes

### Materials Needed

**Essential** (pigments — pick 4 or 5):
- Charcoal from a cold fire, or a soft charcoal stick (black)
- Turmeric powder (brilliant yellow)
- Paprika (orange-red)
- Spinach or grass, mashed hard in a little water (green — chlorophyll)
- Blueberries or a crushed red cabbage leaf (purple — anthocyanin)
- Dirt, dry red clay, or a crushed piece of old brick (ochre)

**Essential** (binders):
- Water (the control — no binder at all)
- Egg yolk, separated, beaten smooth with a splash of water (tempera)
- Cooking oil (a stubborn, slow-drying oil paint)
- White school glue, thinned (a stand-in for modern acrylic polymer)

**Essential** (kit):
- A pestle and mortar, or a sturdy bowl and the back of a spoon
- Small bowls or a muffin tray for mixing
- Thick paper or card, ruled into a grid: pigments down the side, binders across the top
- Brushes or cotton buds

**Optional**:
- Lemon juice and bicarbonate of soda — drip them onto the red cabbage or blueberry paint and watch anthocyanin flip color; it's a pH indicator as well as a pigment
- A magnifier to look at the ground particles
- A blue feather or an iridescent beetle wing to compare against a pigment (structural color)
- Printed comparison images: [Lascaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lascaux), a [blue morpho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morpho_menelaus), [cochineal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochineal) insects on a cactus

**Cost**: Free to Under $5 (kitchen staples)

### Step-by-Step Instructions

**Preparation**:
1. Rule the grid on the card and label it — one row per pigment, one column per binder
2. Set up the mortar and pigment materials on a covered surface (turmeric stains; that's part of its charm and its problem)
3. Separate the egg yolk and beat it; thin the glue; pour a little oil
4. Have a rag and a bowl of water ready for the later splash test

**Activity Flow**:

1. **Introduction — what color actually is** (3-4 min):
   Hold up a turmeric jar. "Why is this yellow? Because it's full of a molecule called curcumin, and curcumin *eats* blue light. White light from the window has every color in it. The curcumin swallows the blue and the violet, and throws back the yellow. So the yellow you're seeing is the leftovers — the light this powder didn't want."
   Point at a leaf: "That's chlorophyll doing the same trick. It eats red light and blue light to make the plant's food, and green is the one color it can't use. Every green leaf you've ever seen is a plant rejecting green."

2. **Exploration — extracting pigment** (8-12 min):
   Grind each material as fine as you can. This is real work and children love it. Narrate what grinding is for: "The finer the powder, the smoother the paint. Painters in the Renaissance had apprentices whose whole job was grinding pigment all day."
   - Charcoal crushes to a satiny black — this is essentially the same carbon black cave painters used.
   - Turmeric and paprika are already ground; just look at them under the magnifier.
   - Spinach or grass needs mashing with a splash of water; the green that comes out is chlorophyll leaving the cells.
   - Blueberries crush into a purple juice; that's anthocyanin.
   - Dirt or brick needs the most muscle and rewards it with real ochre.

3. **Discovery — the binder experiment** (8-10 min):
   Split each pigment into four little piles and stir one into each binder. Paint the grid: every pigment × every binder. Talk while you go:
   "*Pigment* gives the color. *Binder* makes it stick. Watch — the water one looks fine right now. Let's see what it looks like when it dries."
   Note the differences as they appear: egg yolk paint goes glossy and dries fast; oil stays wet for ages and creeps into the paper; glue dries plasticky and slightly raised; water-only sits dull on top.
   Then let it dry (10 minutes at least; oil will still be tacky). **Run the test**: rub each square with a finger, then splash a drop of water on each and dab it.
   - Water alone: the pigment lifts straight off onto your finger. Beautiful color, not paint.
   - Egg tempera: tough and stubborn. This is why 700-year-old egg-tempera altarpieces still look bright.
   - Glue: waterproof and shiny.
   - Oil: still smudgy today, rock-hard in a week.

4. **Wrap-up — pigments in everything** (3-4 min):
   Go around the room and name pigments in living things: "Your hair and your skin are colored by *melanin* — the more melanin, the darker, and it's there to soak up ultraviolet light from the sun so it can't damage your cells. Blood is red because of *haemoglobin*, which has an iron atom in the middle of it — the same iron as in a nail, and as in the rust that makes ochre red. Flamingos are born grey and turn pink from eating shrimp full of carotene — *the pigment you eat becomes the pigment you are*. Carrots have the same one."
   Then the twist, holding up a blue feather or a picture of a morpho butterfly: "But this blue isn't a pigment at all. There's no blue paint in this feather. It's covered in ridges too small to see that bounce the light so only blue survives. That's *structural color*. Crush the feather and the blue disappears — you get plain brown dust."

### Age Adaptations

**2-3 years**:
- Grinding is the whole activity. Let them pound charcoal to dust for as long as they want.
- Use just two pigments (turmeric, charcoal) and two binders (water, glue) so the comparison is stark.
- Name the pigment every time you use it: "That's *turmeric*. That's *curcumin* inside it."
- Do the finger-rub test with them — the smear of dry pigment lifting off is a very satisfying result at this age.
- Watch the sink: the turmeric handprint on a white towel is a free demonstration of what "no binder" means.

**3-4+ years**:
- Add the pH experiment: paint a stripe of blueberry or red-cabbage pigment, then drip lemon juice on one end and bicarbonate solution on the other. Pink one way, blue-green the other. "Anthocyanin doesn't just show you color — it tells you whether something is an acid or a base."
- Test lightfastness: put half of a finished grid on a sunny windowsill and half in a drawer for two weeks. Turmeric fades dramatically; charcoal doesn't move. "Museums keep the lights low for exactly this reason."
- Tell the money stories: [ultramarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultramarine) came from lapis lazuli hauled out of one mountain range in Afghanistan and cost more than gold, which is why medieval painters saved it for the Virgin Mary's robe. [Tyrian purple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrian_purple) came from a gland in sea snails — thousands of snails per gram — and only emperors could afford it, which is why "royal purple" is a phrase at all. [Cochineal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochineal) red comes from crushed insects and is still in some sweets and lipsticks; check a label together.
- Compare subtractive mixing (pigments; every added color absorbs more light, so mixtures go dark) with additive mixing (light; a screen adds red, green, and blue light to make white). Look at a white patch of a phone screen through a magnifier and find the three colored dots.

### Extension Ideas

**Variation 1 — Cave Painter's Kit**: Ochre plus animal fat (butter works) plus a hand stencil. Mix earth pigment with fat, blow or dab it around a hand held flat against paper, and lift. That is, materially, the same paint used 30,000 years ago.

**Variation 2 — Dye vs. Pigment**: Boil onion skins, red cabbage, or beetroot and soak a strip of white cloth. A dye bonds *into* the fibre; a pigment sits *on top* held by a binder. Wash both and see which survives.

**Variation 3 — The Autumn Reveal**: Take a green leaf, chop it, soak it in a little rubbing alcohol, and stand a strip of coffee filter in it for an hour. The pigments crawl up the paper and separate — green chlorophyll, yellow xanthophyll, orange carotene, all of which were hiding in the leaf the whole time. This is chromatography, and it explains autumn.

**Repeat play**: Keep a jar for "pigment finds" — a rusty stone, a lump of chalk, red brick dust, charcoal from a barbecue, walnut husks. Each new find is a paint waiting to be made.

## 📝 Parent/Caregiver Notes

### Common Challenges & Solutions

**Challenge**: Turmeric stains the counter, the towel, and the child.
**Solution**: It will. Cover surfaces, use old clothes, and treat the stains as the lesson: "This one stains because the pigment particles are tiny enough to lodge in the fibres." Baking soda paste and sunlight lift turmeric off most surfaces; skin fades in a day.

**Challenge**: The child wants to eat the pigments.
**Solution**: Use kitchen materials and this stops being frightening — most of them *are* food. Say clearly which is which: "Turmeric goes in curry. Charcoal and dirt do not go in mouths." Skip charcoal and soil entirely for a determined mouther and stick to spice and berry pigments.

**Challenge**: The paint looks disappointing next to shop paint.
**Solution**: That's a genuine finding, not a failure. Homemade pigment is coarse; shop pigment is milled for hours. Look at both under the magnifier and compare particle size. "Ours is gritty. Theirs is like flour. That's the only difference."

**Challenge**: The egg yolk paint smells and the child objects.
**Solution**: Mix it fresh and use it within the session; add a drop of vinegar to slow it down. Or skip yolk and use glue and oil — the comparison still works with three binders.

**Challenge**: The child just wants to paint a picture rather than fill in the grid.
**Solution**: Let them. Do the grid first with two squares, then hand over the pots. The rub test works just as well on a painting of a dinosaur.

### Follow-Up Activities

1. **Read the labels**: Look for pigment names on a paint tube, a crayon box, or a food packet — titanium dioxide, iron oxide, carmine, curcumin, beta-carotene. They are the same molecules you just ground up.

2. **Name pigments on sight**: Green leaf, chlorophyll. Brown skin, melanin. Red blood, haemoglobin. Orange carrot, carotene. Yellow autumn leaf, xanthophyll. After a week of this, the child will start pointing them out unprompted.

3. **Visit an old painting**: In a gallery or a book, find a picture over 400 years old and look for the blue. Ask what it cost.
