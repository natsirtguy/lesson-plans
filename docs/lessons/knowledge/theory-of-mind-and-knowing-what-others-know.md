# 📚 KNOWLEDGE, SKILLS & CULTURE: Theory of Mind and Knowing What Others Know

## 🎯 Activity Summary

Other people's heads contain *different things than yours*. That sounds obvious, but it is one of the hardest ideas a human brain ever has to build — and children under about four genuinely do not have it yet. This lesson teaches **theory of mind**: the understanding that another person has their own beliefs, knowledge, and desires, which can be *wrong* or simply different. Children run the two most famous experiments in developmental psychology on themselves (the hidden-toy **false-belief test** and the broccoli-and-crackers preference test), then put the idea to work by describing a treasured object to an audience that has never seen it.

The point is not to make the child "pass" the test. A 2-year-old will fail it, and watching that failure — narrating it out loud — is the lesson. This ability grows in over months, like teeth.

## Vocabulary

**Theory of mind**: Understanding that other people have minds of their own — their own knowledge, beliefs, feelings, and plans — and that what is in their head is not the same as what is in yours. It is called a *theory* because you can never see inside another mind; you can only build a model of it from the outside and predict what they'll do. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_mind))

**False belief**: Something a person thinks is true that isn't — usually because they missed information you have. If Dad puts his keys on the table and you move them to the shelf while he's out, Dad now has a *false belief* about where his keys are. He will look on the table. Predicting that is the classic test of theory of mind, and most children start passing it between ages 3 and 5. ([Sally–Anne test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sally%E2%80%93Anne_test))

**Perspective-taking**: Deliberately working out what someone else sees, knows, or feels from where *they* are standing. There is a visual kind (what can you see from over there?) and a knowledge kind (what do you already know about my toy?). The visual kind comes first, around age 2; the knowledge kind takes years longer.

**Knowledge asymmetry**: When two people know different amounts about the same thing. Every explanation in the world exists because of knowledge asymmetry — a teacher knows what a student doesn't, a presenter knows their object and the audience doesn't. Good explaining means constantly estimating the gap.

**Egocentrism**: Not selfishness — the developmental stage, named by Jean Piaget, where a child assumes everyone knows what they know and sees what they see. A 2-year-old on the phone will nod their head to answer a question. That is egocentrism, and it is completely normal.

## Activity

### Activity Overview

Three parts, escalating: first a live false-belief experiment with a hidden toy, then a test of whether the child can act on someone else's *preference* instead of their own, and finally show-and-tell reframed as an engineering problem — "my audience has never seen this; what do they need from me?" Every part is a real experiment from the research literature, run on a real child.

**Activity Type**: Live psychology experiment + communication practice

**Duration**: 20-25 minutes (three parts; any one part stands alone)

**Setup time**: 5 minutes

### Materials Needed

**Essential**:
- Two visibly different containers or hiding places (a box and a basket; two upturned bowls)
- A small toy the child cares about
- A second person to play the one who "leaves the room" (a stuffed animal works if no second adult is available — narrate for it)
- Two snack foods: one the child loves (crackers, raisins) and one the child dislikes (raw broccoli, a lemon wedge, olives)
- A treasured object of the child's choosing for the show-and-tell portion

**Optional**:
- An opaque bag for the "guess the object" variation
- A phone to record predictions so you can replay the same experiment months later and see the change
- A blindfold or a scarf for the "what can you see from there?" version

**Cost**: Free (uses household items)

### Step-by-Step Instructions

**Preparation**:
1. Choose two obviously different hiding places and set them side by side
2. Brief your second person: they will watch the toy get hidden, leave the room, and come back looking for it
3. Have both snack foods ready on separate plates; taste-check that the child really does dislike one of them
4. Decide now that there is no wrong answer today — you are collecting data, not correcting

**Activity Flow**:

1. **Introduction** (2-3 min):
   Sit facing your child and say the real idea out loud: "You have thoughts inside your head. I have thoughts inside *my* head. And here is the strange part — they are not the same thoughts. I can't see yours and you can't see mine. Working out what's inside someone else's head is called *theory of mind*, and human brains take about four years to build it. Let's find out how yours is coming along."

2. **Experiment 1 — the false-belief test** (5-7 min):
   With your helper watching, let the child hide the toy in the box. Now the helper leaves ("I'm going to get a drink"). While they're gone, move the toy — loudly, obviously — into the basket. Then ask the child the question that matters:

   *"When they come back, where will they look for the toy?"*

   Most 2- and 3-year-olds will say **the basket** — where the toy actually is — because they cannot yet hold "what I know" separately from "what they know." Do not correct them. Bring your helper back in and let the child watch them walk straight to the box. Then narrate it: "Look! They looked in the box. They didn't know we moved it. Their head still has the old idea in it. That's a *false belief*."
   Run it again with roles swapped: send the *child* out of the room, move the toy, and ask what they thought when they came back.

3. **Experiment 2 — broccoli and crackers** (5 min):
   This one is from a famous 1997 study by Betty Repacholi and Alison Gopnik. Put both plates out. Taste the food the child loves and make a disgusted face: "Ugh! Yuck!" Then taste the food they dislike and light up: "Mmm! Delicious!" Now hold out your open hand and say, in a neutral voice, "Can you give me some?"

   Watch what happens. Younger children hand over their *own* favorite — they can only model one set of preferences, theirs. Around 18 months and up, some children will hand over the broccoli: they've worked out that your wants are different from their wants. Either result is worth saying out loud: "You gave me the crackers because *you* love crackers. But I made a yuck face at those! What did I like?"

4. **Experiment 3 — show and tell, done properly** (5-8 min):
   Now use the idea. The child picks a treasured object and describes it to someone who has never seen it. Set the challenge explicitly: **"I have never seen this in my life. My head is empty about it. What do I need to know?"**
   - When they say something vague ("it's nice"), push on the gap: "My head still doesn't have a picture. Nice how? Nice to touch?"
   - When they nail a detail, name what they did: "Bumpy — now I can picture it. You just put something into my head that wasn't there before."
   - Then close the loop: "What did I know before you talked? What do I know now?" That difference *is* the knowledge asymmetry, and closing it is what explaining means.

5. **Wrap-up** (2 min):
   "Today you found out that other people's heads have different stuff in them. Sometimes they're missing something you know — that's a *false belief*. Sometimes they want something different from what you want. And when you tell somebody about your toy, you're putting a picture from your head into theirs." Note the date and their answers somewhere; this is a test worth repeating in six months.

### Age Adaptations

**2-3 years**:
- Expect them to fail the false-belief test, loudly and confidently. That is the finding, not a problem. Never say "no, that's wrong" — say "let's watch and see!"
- Do the *visual* perspective-taking version instead, which they can often do: hold a picture facing you and ask "can you see the picture? Can I?" Then sit on opposite sides of a tall book and ask what each of you can see.
- Play peekaboo and hide-and-seek deliberately and notice the tell: toddlers "hide" by covering their own eyes — if they can't see you, they assume you can't see them. Name it: "You covered your eyes! You thought I'd disappear too."
- Keep show-and-tell to one sentence. Even "It's soft!" is a real contribution to another mind.

**3-4+ years**:
- Run the full false-belief test and then the harder second-order version: "Where does *she* think *he* will look?"
- Add the deceptive-container test: show a familiar box (a raisin box) that you've secretly filled with pebbles. Ask what's inside; open it; then ask, "Your friend hasn't seen inside. What will *they* say is in it?" Children who haven't built theory of mind yet say "pebbles" — and often insist they thought pebbles all along.
- Connect it to lying and surprises: you can only plan a surprise party, or tell a lie, if you can model what someone else believes. "A surprise only works because you know something they don't."
- Try the blind-description game: the child hides the object behind their back and describes it from memory; you draw what you hear; compare the drawing to the object. Every mismatch is a gap in the description.

### Extension Ideas

**Variation 1 — The Cracker Experiment in the Wild**: At snack time, deliberately want something different from what the child wants and ask them to fetch it. "I would like the sour one, please." Watch whether they bring theirs or yours.

**Variation 2 — Guess What's in the Bag**: Put an object in an opaque bag. The presenter gives clues without naming it; the audience guesses. This forces the presenter to model exactly how much the guesser knows and add just enough.

**Variation 3 — Two Views of One Scene**: Sit across a table from each other with a toy scene between you. Take a photo from each side and compare. "You saw the front. I saw the back. Same toys, different heads."

**Repeat play**: Rerun the false-belief test every couple of months with the same toy and the same containers. There will come a week when the answer flips. Write down the date — you have watched a mind change shape.

## 📝 Parent/Caregiver Notes

### Common Challenges & Solutions

**Challenge**: The child fails the false-belief test and an adult corrects them or laughs.
**Solution**: Decide before you start that the "wrong" answer is the interesting one. This is a developmental stage, not a mistake — it's like being annoyed at a baby for not walking. Say "let's watch and find out" and let reality demonstrate.

**Challenge**: The child refuses to give you the food you asked for.
**Solution**: Fine — that's a different result (a want of their own, strongly held). Don't force it. Try again another day, or run it with a stuffed animal who "wants" the broccoli.

**Challenge**: The child is shy about presenting to anyone.
**Solution**: The audience can be one trusted person, a pet, or a stuffed animal — the concept works at any audience size, because it's about the gap in knowledge, not the crowd. Start there and expand only when they want to.

**Challenge**: The child says "it's nice" and won't elaborate no matter how many times you ask.
**Solution**: Stop asking open questions and make the gap concrete instead: close your eyes and say "I can't see it now. Tell me one thing so I can picture it." Then draw what they tell you, badly and wrongly, and let them correct you. Correcting a wrong drawing is much easier than describing into a void.

**Challenge**: An older sibling shouts the answer.
**Solution**: Give the older child the experimenter's job instead. Running the test on someone else — and predicting what the little one will say — is a harder and better exercise for them.

### Follow-Up Activities

1. **Narrate other minds all day**: "The mail carrier doesn't know it's your birthday." "Grandma doesn't know we moved the chairs — watch where she sits." Free, constant, and the most effective version of this lesson.

2. **Read a story with a secret**: Any book where the reader knows something a character doesn't (*Little Red Riding Hood*, *We're Going on a Bear Hunt*) is a false-belief exercise. Pause and ask: "Does she know what we know?"

3. **Plan a real surprise**: Hide something for another family member and keep it secret until they find it. Keeping the secret *is* holding two models of the world at once.
